\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
The Super Ensemble’s 16--18\% skill gain is driven by its ability to navigate the ``Interpolation vs. Extrapolation'' dilemma. The ``Soft Voting'' mechanism allows the system to remain anchored to the Statistical Trend during normal years while shifting weight to Physics-Informed components during extreme years like 2003 and 2018. The fact that the Skill Score improves as climate volatility increases (rising from 16.16\% globally to 18.07\% in the 2010--2024 period) suggests that the regime-switching logic becomes more valuable as historical norms become less reliable.

\subsection{Error Decomposition and the Oracle Gap}
A forensic analysis of the forecasting pipeline reveals an ``Oracle MAE'' of 40.34 dt/ha, representing the theoretical error limit if the Meta-Learner perfectly selected the best available sub-model in every instance. Our current Super Ensemble MAE of 56.35 dt/ha indicates that 71.6\% of the remaining error is attributable to the limitations of the underlying sub-models (the ``Component Gap''), while only 28.4\% stems from selection errors by the Meta-Learner. This suggests that the gating logic is highly efficient, and further improvements in forecasting skill will likely require higher-fidelity process simulations or improved drought-stress parameterization rather than further optimization of the ensemble weighting.

\subsection{Spatial Vulnerabilities: East vs. West}
The system exhibits a higher ``unsolvable'' error (Oracle Error) in Eastern Germany (42.5 dt/ha) compared to the West (39.6 dt/ha). This spatial disparity is likely driven by the continental climate and coarser soil profiles of the East, which amplify non-linear responses to summer heatwaves. These conditions often push the crop beyond the physiological recovery limits assumed in standard WOFOST 7.2 calibrations, highlighting a need for site-specific parameterization in future iterations.

\subsection{The Lead-Time Advantage}
A critical contribution of this work is addressing the ``Lead-Time Gap'' in operational agricultural monitoring. Standard systems like the European Commission’s MCYFS \citep{vandervelde2019mars} rely heavily on observed satellite and meteorological data, which only become predictive as the growing season progresses. In contrast, our Super Ensemble targets the \textbf{March 1st window}. By extracting a directional heat signal ($r=0.63$) from spring precursors months before planting, the system provides a robust early-warning mechanism that complements, rather than competes with, monitoring-based systems.

\subsection{Uncertainty and Risk Mitigation}
The 43.1\% error reduction during the 2018 drought is the most significant validation of the Regret-Weighted training scheme. By prioritizing training instances where the Statistical Trend failed catastrophically, the Meta-Learner learned to recognize the bio-physical precursor patterns (e.g., the interaction of winter water recharge and spring solar radiation) that precede yield crashes. For risk managers, this ability to mitigate ``Black Swan'' failures is often more valuable than marginal gains in average accuracy.

\subsection{Limitations}
Despite the promising results, limitations remain. The inherent conservatism of machine learning—observed in the under-prediction of absolute heat peaks in Fig \ref{fig:heat_calibration}—means the model may still struggle to capture the full magnitude of record-breaking compound extremes. Furthermore, while the noise filter ($\epsilon_{\text{oracle}} > 200$ dt/ha) helps the model focus on climatic signals, it may mask rare, non-climatic systemic failures (e.g., new pest outbreaks) that a truly comprehensive system should eventually incorporate.

\subsection{Forecast vs. Mechanism}
The success of the \textbf{Multivariate Heat Signal} confirms that land-surface memory (soil moisture and temperature) provides a predictive bridge across the seasonal forecast horizon. By using Spring indicators to predict Summer stress, the model bypasses the low skill of raw summer precipitation forecasts. This confirms that hybrid models should not just use process-models as features, but should actively seek out the physical mechanisms that provide early-season predictability.
