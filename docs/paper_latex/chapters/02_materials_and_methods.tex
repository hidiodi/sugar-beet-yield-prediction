\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Study Area and Datasets}
The study covers sugarbeet cultivation districts in Germany, integrating a multi-source dataset spanning the period 1981--2024. To address the challenge of data availability and scale, we harmonized agronomic records with meteorological reanalysis and seasonal forecasts.

\textbf{Agronomic Data.} Yield data ($Y_{d,t}$) for district $d$ and year $t$ was constructed from two primary sources to ensure long-term consistency. Historical records were sourced from the Thuringian State Institute archives, while modern district-level yields were retrieved from the Federal Statistical Office (Destatis) \citep{destatis2024}. Spatial masking for sugarbeet cultivation areas was performed using the DLR CropMap \citep{dlr_cropmap}, ensuring climate signals are aggregated strictly from relevant agricultural pixels.

\textbf{Meteorological Forcing.} We utilized AgERA5 \citep{agera5} for observed daily surface meteorological data (Temperature, Precipitation, Solar Radiation), which serves as the ground truth for model training. For the operational inference mode (March 1st), we integrated seasonal forecasts from ECMWF SEAS5 \citep{ecmwf_seas5}. To mitigate the coarse resolution and bias of raw seasonal forecasts, we employed an Analog Ensemble approach: SEAS5 anomalies were used to select similar historical weather years from the AgERA5 record to drive the process-based simulation.

\textbf{Environmental Covariates.} To capture large-scale atmospheric variability, we included the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), the Scandinavia Pattern (SCAND), and the Multivariate ENSO Index (MEI). Soil moisture dynamics and land surface temperature were derived from ERA5-Land reanalysis \citep{era5land} to provide a dynamic view of root-zone conditions, supplemented by static soil properties from SoilGrids 250m \citep{hengl2017soilgrids250m}.

\subsubsection{Data Quality Control}
To ensure the system learns from climatic signals rather than data errors, we applied a rigorous outlier detection step. Observations where the minimum error achievable by any component model exceeded a threshold $\epsilon_{\text{oracle}} > 200$ dt/ha were flagged as likely data entry errors or non-climatic failures and excluded from the training set.

\subsection{The Statistical Trend Baseline}
The Statistical Trend Baseline serves as the control model, executed via strict Walk-Forward Validation. For any target year $t$, the forecast is composed of a long-term trend and a short-term residual correction.

The long-term trend is modeled using a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) with a smoothing spline function $f_s(\cdot)$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{\text{trend}}(t) = \alpha + f_s(t)
\end{equation}
Subsequently, the residuals from this trend are modeled using an ARIMA(1, 0, 0) process to capture year-to-year autocorrelation. The final baseline forecast is the sum of these components:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{\text{base}}(t) = \hat{y}_{\text{trend}}(t) + \hat{y}_{\text{resid}}(t)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Feature Selection and Engineering}
We employ a two-stage feature engineering pipeline to translate raw environmental data into agronomically meaningful signals, specifically designed to detect physiological stress limits.

\subsubsection{Bio-Physical Risk Scanner}
Implemented within the WOFOST simulation \citep{boogaard1998wofost}, this module calculates risk proxies based on daily interactions between weather and soil state. \textit{Oxygen Stress} ($D_{\text{anoxia}}$) is defined as the count of days in June, July, and August where simulated soil moisture exceeds field capacity, inhibiting root respiration. \textit{Harvest Respiration} is calculated as the accumulated Growing Degree Days (GDD) above 10$^\circ$C during the harvest window.

\subsubsection{Climate Indices and Stress Vectors}
To capture the magnitude of extreme events without introducing data sparsity, we utilize a recursive Z-score normalization. For a feature $x$ at year $t$, the Z-score is calculated using only the historical mean $\mu_{1:t-1}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{1:t-1}$ to prevent data leakage:
\begin{equation}
    Z_t = \frac{x_t - \mu_{1:t-1}}{\sigma_{1:t-1}}
\end{equation}
Key inputs normalized via this method include forecasted heat days ($Z_{\text{heat}}$), summer water balance ($Z_{\text{bal}}$), and effective winter recharge ($Z_{\text{tank}}$).

These normalized signals are combined into a \textbf{Compound Failure Index} ($I_{\text{fail}}$) that aggregates three distinct stress vectors: Scorch, Waterlogging, and Late Sowing.
\begin{align}
    I_{\text{scorch}} &= \max\left(Z_{\text{heat}} \cdot (-Z_{\text{bal}})^+ , \ (Z_{\text{heat}} - 1.5)^+ \cdot 2.0\right) \\
    I_{\text{log}} &= (Z_{\text{anoxia}} - 0.8)^+ \cdot 2.0 \\
    I_{\text{late}} &= (Z_{\text{sow}} - 1.5)^+ \cdot 2.0 \\
    I_{\text{fail}} &= \max(I_{\text{scorch}}, I_{\text{log}}, I_{\text{late}})
\end{align}
Conversely, we define an index for ideal conditions, $I_{\text{bumper}}$, as the interaction between adequate water supply and cooler temperatures.

\subsubsection{Biological Proxies}
We further derive proxies for biotic stress, including $D_{\text{mild}}$ (days with $T_{\text{min}} > 0^\circ$C in Jan/Feb) and $D_{\text{fungal}}$ (days with $T_{\text{max}} > 15^\circ$C and precipitation $> 1$ mm in May/June). Root zone depletion ($R_{\text{dep}}$) quantifies the mismatch between atmospheric demand and soil supply, calculated as the ratio of summer heat demand to winter water recharge.

\subsection{Predictive Algorithms}
We implemented the machine learning components using Scikit-Learn \citep{scikitlearn} and XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost}.

\textbf{Multivariate Heat Signal.} Designed to address the early-season information deficit, this model predicts long-term heat exposure using an XGBoost Regressor. To prioritize the learning of rare events, we apply a cubic weighting scheme to the loss function:
\begin{equation}
    w_t = \min\left(50, \left(\frac{h_t}{\bar{h}}\right)^3\right)
\end{equation}
where $h_t$ is the observed heat days for year $t$ and $\bar{h}$ is the long-term average. This focuses the model on years with extreme high-temperature signals (e.g., 2003).

\textbf{Physics-Informed Ensemble.} This component trains two Gradient Boosting models targeting the 0.20 and 0.80 quantiles, defining the ``Pessimistic Floor'' and ``Optimistic Ceiling'' of the yield distribution. It employs a logic that blends the statistical trend with the quantile predictions based on the simulated WOFOST biomass ratio.

\textbf{Hybrid XGBoost.} Following the ``Process-Guided'' paradigm \citep{shahhosseini2021coupling}, this model uses WOFOST simulation outputs as input features rather than final predictors. It targets the yield ratio $R_t = Y_t / \hat{y}_{\text{trend}}(t)$. To capture uncertainty, distinct models are trained for the 0.025, 0.50, and 0.975 quantiles.

\textbf{Robust Linear Integrator.} This component uses a HuberRegressor ($\epsilon=1.35$) to predict the yield residual. It calculates an internal linear trend separate from the global GAM baseline to maintain statistical independence. Inputs include the WOFOST residual, root zone depletion ($R_{\text{dep}}$), and biological risk proxies ($D_{\text{mild}}$, $D_{\text{fungal}}$).

\subsection{Super Ensemble Architecture}
The final forecasting system is a Super Ensemble driven by a Meta-Learner (XGBoost Classifier). This Meta-Learner functions as a dynamic gating network, assigning weights to the specialized sub-models based on the detected climatic regime.

The Meta-Learner is trained on a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{d,t}$ comprising four categories: ensemble variance (uncertainty proxy), historical trend bias, bio-physical context ($Z_{\text{heat}}$, $Z_{\text{anoxia}}$), and the magnitude of the current trend forecast deviation. The classifier outputs a probability distribution over the available regimes. The final yield forecast $\hat{Y}_{d,t}$ is calculated as the probability-weighted sum of the component predictions via soft voting:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{Y}_{d,t} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(M_k | \mathbf{x}_{d,t}) \cdot \hat{y}_k(d,t)
\end{equation}
where $P(M_k | \mathbf{x}_{d,t})$ is the probability assigned to component model $M_k$. This architecture allows for a continuous transition between the ``Trend Regime'' and the ``Physics Regime'' rather than a binary switch.

\subsection{Implementation}
The system is designed for operational deployment on March 1st. While the WOFOST simulation requires daily weather data processing, the component models are computationally efficient, allowing for rapid district-level inference across the study area.
